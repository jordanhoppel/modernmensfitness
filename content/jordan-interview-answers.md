**Q1. Coaching philosophy (two sentences)**
Principles are few, methods are many. The methods I use are dictated by each person's individual situation, and everyone has uniqueness about them.

**Q2. Who do you coach best, and who is a bad fit?**
Best: people willing to work hard, learn, and be open to suggestion and to trying things that may seem unconventional but are rooted in sound logic and first-principles thinking. Bad fit: people who are arrogant, cocky, think they know it all, and are lazy and don't work hard.

**Q3. One thing every lifter should understand in their first year**
How important the fundamentals of lifting are, and how important it is to perfect them before moving on to anything more advanced. Beginners get rapid gains early, get excited, and assume they'll keep advancing at that rate. That's just not the case.

**Q4. What do you do differently from most online programs or coaches?**
I come with a full coaching suite: in-person coaching, a training app, and online coaching. My background is fairly unique; I was an All-American sprinter in college. A lot of online coaches have never trained people in person. I have trained people almost exclusively in person.

**Q5. First three questions when someone doesn't know what program to pick**
1. What are your top three goals, in order?
2. What have you been doing leading up to now?
3. What is your schedule and availability to train? I don't want them committing to a schedule they can't completely fulfill.

**Q6. Rules for matching a program to a goal**
These are really rules of exercise science and biology more than my rules.
- Strength: more sets, fewer reps, take advantage of recovery, and don't go to failure often, to keep the neurological system fresh.
- Hypertrophy: similar methods, but you take the muscle into mechanical tension and get close to failure.
- Fat loss: energy out must be greater than energy in. Keep protein high to hold onto muscle and to help stick to the diet, since protein keeps you full longer.

**Q7. 3, 4, or 5 training days**
Depends on goal, schedule, training age, and experience. Beginners stay toward 3-4 days; more advanced people toward 5. For strength, even an inexperienced person may train every day, but not with super high volume each day, because strength wants high exposure to lifting and sets. Higher frequency that you can recover from easily favors strength; hypertrophy is more about maximally recovering from every session. As long as someone can keep up with the schedule, recover, and continue to make gains, that's what they should do. They need to be able to do the sessions well, have energy in them, and perform.

**Q8. Common mistakes picking a program**
Most people don't really pick a program. They follow random advice from social media or people with good physiques. That advice isn't always bad, but it isn't rooted in biomechanics or individual differences. A strength coach has seen many archetypes, so a coach's experience is a much sounder approach. People pick on vibes; they should look at who is writing the program, that person's experience level, and whether it fits their goals. Coaches who give everyone the same program with nothing individualized: "it's cheap, it's dishonest." People need some understanding of strength training and exercise physiology so they don't get steered down the wrong path.

**Q9. How long to stick with a program; people who keep switching**
8 to 12 weeks for a first program. If you only do week one of every program, you never see how it's built to progress; in a periodized program, reps, sets, and exercises change from first week to last for a reason. The body needs time for tissues to adapt. Early on, most progress is neurological, not muscular (people often attribute it to the muscle), so it takes longer for changes to take hold in the muscle. Once training age is higher and training is consistent, readjustment after time off is much quicker. "The rebound is really quick."

**Q11. True beginner**
Someone who has never played a sport, done anything physical with their body, or formally lifted weights. Gym class in middle or high school doesn't count.

**Q12. What a beginner needs before starting**
Nothing tangible. If training with a coach or in a gym: appropriate clothing and footwear, a good attitude, an open approach, and high trait openness to training, coaching, and working out.

**Q13. Beginner week**
No standard beginner week, since goals differ. The first week is exploratory: seeing how a person moves naturally without my guidance, their natural tendencies in movement patterns, and what they're capable of before I give instruction. I take them through about 5 to 15 exercises or machines, 1 to 3 sets each, noting performance, whether they like it, and form, to get a baseline for programming.

**Q14. Adding weight as a beginner**
Add 5 pounds, maximum 10 pounds per set. How often depends on the program, progress, and goals: it can happen every set, every week, or every month.

**Q15. When a beginner stalls**
Usually it's form-related: a technique flaw. They aren't positioning the body correctly, aren't using the correct muscles in the right order or context, movements aren't coordinated or smooth, or they're in an open-loop movement pattern when they need a closed-loop one. "It really just depends."

**Q16. First lifts and their order**
Depends completely on goals. I teach movement patterns more than lifts, based on experience level, training age, overall age, and injuries. I want to see: squat, hinge, lunge, overhead press, overhead pull, horizontal press and pull, and a lying-on-the-back pushing pattern, to see natural tendencies. The specific lifts used depend on experience level.

**Q17. Progressive overload to a new client**
Adjusting your training based on the level of improvement you are actively experiencing.

**Q18. Favorite ways to add weight or reps, ranked**
If you can do 5 reps with 5 pounds on day one and you're stronger by week four, add weight or add reps; both are progressive overload. If you use the same load forever, your body has no stress to adapt to and no reason to improve. Ranking: first add more reps, then add more weight.

**Q19. When adding weight every week stops working**
Assuming they're not overtrained and are properly recovered (a pure training plateau), advance them into training that challenges the nervous system. First steps: slow the eccentric tempo, or pause the lift at the maximal lengthened position for 3 seconds, then push through.

**Q20. Realistic 8-week progression**
Start with higher overall reps and volume (can come from tempo too). As the 8 weeks progress, weights get heavier, overall volume decreases, and sets potentially increase. Toward the end, mix in faster, dynamic lifts to drive the neurological system. At the end of a strength program you move light weight very fast, and move maximal weight for very low reps, potentially in different ranges of motion.

**Q21. Days per week**
Depends on level and goal. For most people 3 to 5 times a week is enough. At 3 days, usually full body (upper and lower lifts the same day, not every muscle). At 4 or 5 days, a split: push/pull/legs, upper/lower, or muscle group by muscle group.

**Q22. 3-day vs. 5-day split**
No correct answer; the person's individual situation matters more.

**Q23. Rest days**
No set number; depends on the goal. Someone training for a marathon doesn't have to take fully off days. Someone training for competition pushes through differently than someone just staying in good shape. Training frequency is based on how quickly you can recover; it cannot outpace recovery if you want continued quality progress.

**Q24. Percentages of one-rep max**
Training percentage is lifting intensity. It is not aggression or effort, which people often assume. The percentage of your one-rep max gives a rep range you should be able to do. Example: a 325 max, 70% is right around 250 pounds, and someone could generally do 5 to 8 reps there.

**Q25. Percentage ranges for strength vs. size**
Mostly the same percentages. In strength work you're not trying to get to failure; it's task-driven (a certain weight for a certain number of reps). In hypertrophy you're going for mechanical tension and getting close to failure. The more you go to failure in strength training, the more it works against you and the more you plateau; you want to complete the programmed reps.

**Q26. Rep ranges**
- Strength: 3 to 5. The "three to five method": 3-5 exercises, 3-5 sets, 3-5 reps, 3-5 days a week.
- Hypertrophy: typically 6 to 12, very individual. Proximity to failure is easier to measure around 6-8 than 10-12. Can go as low as 5 or as high as 30 reps as long as proximity to failure is the same. People think they need higher reps than they do because the pump and blood flow feel like it's working more, but mechanical tension is the main driver, and the closer to failure, the higher the tension.
- Endurance: 30 reps and over, more aerobic than anaerobic.

**Q27. Testing true maxes**
Yes, for clients with a specific goal to train their one-rep max and hit a particular number. Otherwise I use a 3-rep or 5-rep max as a proxy for whether they've gotten stronger lift to lift. Right around 3 to 5 reps; above that the numbers aren't very reliable.

**Q28. Deload week**
Drop the volume. Keep intensity generally the same, but cut volume in half.

**Q29. How you know someone needs a deload**
They report poor sleep, training feels like a grind, they feel worn out, and their numbers trend downward or haven't trended upward in a very long time. (In maintenance or in-season phases you're not trying to get stronger, just to recover from game to game.)

**Q30. Why a squat stops going up**
Overtrained, under-recovered, or an underlying injury they don't realize is holding them back. Could be at their genetic limit, but that's rare outside professional lifters or bodybuilders. Generally, their nervous system is fatigued.

**Q31. From a 225 squat toward 315**
If someone already squats 225, they've spent some time in the gym, or have good genetics for handling training volume. I don't know how long it will take; it depends on training age, genetics, and how good they are.
- First 2-4 weeks: make sure technique is sound and they can squat to multiple depths and feel all the depths.
- Then a high-set, low-rep, high-tempo progression, e.g. 10 sets of 6 or 10 sets of 4 with a 4-5 second eccentric.
- Example: 135 lb lifter with a 225 max. First 4 sets at 135 for 4 reps with a 4-second eccentric, then the next 6 sets in pairs, increasing 5-10 lb every two sets. Same for the first two weeks.
- After that, drop to 8 sets, same reps.
- Next 4 weeks: tempo plus a one-and-a-quarter squat (go all the way down to the lowest good-form depth, come up a quarter, drop back to the bottom, then all the way up). 8 sets of 5 for the first 2 weeks, then 6 sets of 3 for the next 2 weeks.
- Final progression: drop the eccentric from 5 seconds to 3 seconds, keep the quarter squat for the first 2 weeks of that block, then cut the tempo and quarter squat for the last 2 weeks, keeping 8 sets and dropping reps to 2. [unclear: Jordan said "the final 12 weeks"; this may mean the final block of the plan, or the whole plan running 12 weeks]
- Week 13: test the squat.
- Mapping: going up 5 lb every set is feasible; treat the second-to-last set as the previous week's heaviest set, and progress by one or two weight units (one unit = 5 lb, next = 10 lb) to project where they'd end up after 12 weeks.

**Q32. What to track**
Track what's pertinent, that you can do something about: sets, reps, rest time, tempo, the lifts used, how you felt that day, anything that gives context on the session. If it's not in your control it's probably not worth tracking; it will cause more harm than good, because you shouldn't worry about things you can't control.

**Q33. Measuring getting stronger**
If the weights they're moving continually go up according to the schedule and progression they should be following, every single week, they're getting stronger.

**Q34. What makes a good lifting app**
The programs and intelligence should come from a coach with experience, not a large language model. LLMs have no skin in the game and miss information a coach picks up on. AI is fine for menial, time-consuming tasks and organization, but having AI intelligence behind fitness is not smart; it lacks the nodes necessary and has no skin in the game. What annoys me: most legacy lifting apps are like BlackBerry compared to Apple; they're clunky, with legacy settings that aren't customizable, so each person doesn't get a good experience.

**Q35. Spreadsheet vs. app**
A spreadsheet only wins if you don't want technology around and don't want to be distracted by a phone. On data, organization, and capabilities, an app always wins because it has built-in calculations that go far beyond a clunky spreadsheet.

**Q36. Protein**
A good proxy that's easy to follow: a gram of protein per pound of body weight.

**Q37. Calories to gain muscle**
Estimate maintenance calories, then increase by 300 to 500.

**Q38. Protein timing**
No, the post-workout window doesn't matter.

**Q39. Foods recommended most**
The one thing I specifically recommend: get complete proteins. All animal-derived proteins are complete, with all essential and non-essential amino acids. The body can make some amino acids but not all, so you need complete sources (or the aminos on their own), but the body does better when they come together.

**Q40. Meal prep**
I don't meal prep that often. When I do, I cook and season proteins so they're quick to reheat, since protein takes time to cook, and use rice I can microwave quickly. Tell busy clients: have food at home that you know you're eating that day. If they're unprepared at the end of the day, hungry, they grab the closest thing, often comfort food or fast food.

**Q41. Quick high-protein meals (10 minutes or less)**
Beef, chicken, or bison with rice or potatoes.

**Q42. Sleep**
Sleep is critically important for everything to do with fitness. Get as much sleep as you possibly can; more sleep can only benefit your fitness goals.

**Q43. Tips for bad sleepers**
Get into a routine. Temperature matters: keep the air low and crisp. Take a hot shower at night so the body cools itself afterward, which is good for sleep. Avoid lots of lights. Eat enough calories and protein during the day so you don't get hunger pangs at night. Lower overall stress at the end of the day and get into a parasympathetic state where the brain isn't actively stressing; reading a book can help. Stay away from screens and bright lights. Supplements such as magnesium, melatonin, and GABA can also help.

**Q44. Muscle soreness**
Soreness is a response a muscle experiences after being placed under stress. It's not necessarily a predictor of a good workout; it's a byproduct of workouts in general.

**Q45. Supplements**
Handle the basics before supplements, and get health advice from your doctor, someone measuring the levels in your body. Ones with a lot of research that I've recommended:
- Whey protein: another complete protein source, an easy way to get protein in when it's hard through the day. They shouldn't be reliant on shakes.
- Creatine: very highly studied; people who want to take it should. Start around 5 g a day and bump up as necessary. Alternatively a loading phase of 10 to 20 g for the first 10 days to saturate the body, then a low maintenance dose. Anecdotal and clinical research suggests up to 20 g a day helps people recoup after a poor night of sleep (brain fog, overall energy), and many recent studies related to Alzheimer's have been positive.

**Q46. How to take creatine (replacement question)**
Take it at a time you'll remember daily; consistency matters. Probably don't take it with certain minerals like calcium, since it may compete for absorption and be less efficient. I typically tell people to take it in the morning because it's easier to remember, or after a workout, or at night.

**Q47. Common creatine questions**
Most are about bloating and weight gain. Creatine can make you put on weight because the body holds more water within the muscle, but it's not fat gain. Any bloating feeling would probably be due to something else, not the creatine. Hair loss: there's no real research behind it; it's user bias. People who work out tend to have higher testosterone, which is generally accompanied by hair loss, and the chance they also take creatine is high, so they're associative factors rather than causative.

**Q48. Creatine for women**
The only distinction: women might not need as much, since women generally tend to be smaller overall than men.

**Q49. Caffeine and pre-workout**
If you want to take it and you feel good when you take it, take it. But if it affects your sleep, don't take it for the workout; that's a poor trade-off. Make sure any caffeine or pre-workout isn't affecting your sleep.

**Q50. One page of advice for a new lifter**
It would be general and boring, because that's the stuff that works.
- Maintain consistency.
- Don't be ridiculously, unbelievably strict, but have a bandwidth you can stay within across life, working out, exercise, sleep, and recovery, without really high peaks and really low troughs. You don't need to be perfect all the time, but manage how far you stray from the maintenance line.
- If you're going to do something, make sure you can do it consistently. Don't make promises to yourself that you can't keep; the stress and mental anguish of beating yourself up over a missed workout or eating poorly isn't worth it. Come back the next day and do it better. You can't recover tomorrow; tomorrow's gone. You can only control right now and try to be better for the future.
- Don't over-progress yourself. Don't progress too fast.
- Always lift with good form. Good form is the foundation of everything, and nothing should be sacrificed and put in place of it for any potential gain.
